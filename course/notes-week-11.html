    Section Week 11: Products of Measure Spaces (5A)

    Subsection Products of \(\sigma\)-algebras

    Thus far everything we have done have involved subsets of the real line. However, if we want to integrate multi-variable functions, or study subsets of \(\mathbf{R^n}\) then we need to define measure for these higher dimensional spaces. The starting point will be to define measures for product spaces, This will allow us to define the product of two measures.
    🔗

    Definition 112. Rectangle.

    Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are sets. A rectangle in \(X \times Y\) is a set of the form \(A \times B\) where \(A \subseteq X\) and \(B \subseteq Y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 113. Products of \(\sigma\)-algebras.

    Suppose \((X,S)\) and \((Y,T)\) are measurable spaces. Then the product \(S \otimes T\) is defined to be the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(X \times Y\) that contains \(\{A \times B: A \in S, B \in T\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 114. Measureable Rectangle.

    A measurable rectangle in \(S \otimes T\) is a set of the form \(A \times B\) where \(A \in S\) and \(B \in T\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Example: Let \(X = Y = \mathbf{R}\text{.}\) Let \(S = \{\emptyset, \mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{R}\setminus \mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{R}\}\) and let \(T = \{\emptyset, \{0\}, \mathbf{R}\setminus \{0\}, \mathbf{R}\}\text{.}\) Determine all measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We now define cross-sections of sets. These play an important role in the definition of product measures.
    🔗

    Definition 115. Cross Sections of Sets.

    Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are sets and \(E \subseteq X \times Y\text{.}\) Then for \(a \in X\) and \(b \in Y\) the cross sections \([E]_{a}\) and \([E]^{b}\) are defined by
    \begin{equation*} [E]_a = \{y \in Y: (a,y) \in E\} \quad \textnormal{ and } \quad [E]^b = \{x \in X: (x,b) \in E\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Example: Let \(X = [1,3] \cup [4,5]\) and \(Y = [1,5]\text{.}\) Suppose \(E = X \times Y\text{.}\) Find \([E]_{2}\text{,}\) \([E]_{3.5}\text{,}\) and \([E]^{3}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 116. Cross Sections of Measurable Sets are Measurable.

    Suppose \(S\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(X\) and \(T\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(Y\text{.}\) If \(E \in S \otimes T\) then \([E]_{a} \in T\) for every \(a \in X\) and \([E]^{b} \in S\) for every \(b \in Y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Let \(U\) denote the collection of subsets of \(X \times Y\) for which the result holds. Note that \([A \times B]_{a} = B\) if \(a \in A\) and \(\emptyset\) if \(a \notin A\) and \([A \times B]^{b} = A\) if \(b \in B\) and \(\emptyset\) if \(b \notin B\text{.}\) Hence \(A \times B \in U\) for all \(A \in S\) and \(B \in T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since \([(X \times Y)\setminus E]_{a} = Y \setminus [E]_{a}\text{,}\) and similarly for cross sections of \(Y\text{,}\) \(U\) is closed under compliments.
    🔗
    Since \([E_{1} \cup E_{2} \cup \ldots]_{a} = [E_{1}]_{a} \cup [E_{2}]_{a} \cup \ldots\text{,}\) and similarly for cross sections of \(Y\text{,}\) \(U\) is closed under countable unions.
    🔗
    Hence \(U\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra that contains all measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\text{,}\) so it contains \(S \otimes T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 117. Cross Sections of Functions.

    Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are sets and \(f:X \times Y \to \mathbf{R}\) is a function. Then for \(a \in X\) and \(b \in Y\text{,}\) the cross section functions \([f]_{a}: Y \to \mathbf{R}\) and \([f]^{b}: X \to \mathbf{R}\) are defined by
    \begin{equation*} [f]_a(y) = f(a,y) \text{ for } y \in Y \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} [f]^b(x) = f(x,b) \text{ for } x \in X \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Example: Suppose \(f: \mathbf{R}\times \mathbf{R}\to \mathbf{R}\) is defined by
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y) = \frac{2x-y}{x^2+y^2}. \end{equation*}
    Find \([f]_{2}\) and \([f]^{-3}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 118. Cross Sections of Measurable Functions are Measurable.

    Suppose \(S\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(X\) and \(T\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(Y\text{.}\) Suppose \(f:X \times Y\) is an \(S \otimes T\) measurable function. Then
    1. \([f]_{a}\) is a \(T\) measurable function on \(Y\) for every \(a \in X\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \([f]^{b}\) is an \(S\) measurable function on \(X\) for every \(b \in Y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We will prove the first statement. The proof of the second is similar. Suppose \(D\) is a Borel subset of \(\mathbf{R}\) and \(a\in X\text{.}\) If \(y \in Y\) then
    \begin{align*} y \in ([f]_a)^{-1}(D) \amp \Leftrightarrow [f]_a(y) \in D\\ \amp \Leftrightarrow f(a,y) \in D \\ \amp \Leftrightarrow (a,y) \in f^{-1}(D)\\ \amp \Leftrightarrow y \in [f^{-1}(D)]_a \end{align*}
    Thus \(([f]_{a})^{-1}(D) = [f^{-1}(D)]_{a}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Because \(f\) is an \(S \otimes T\) measurable function, \(f^{-1}(D) \in S \otimes T\text{.}\) Since cross sections are measurable \([f^{-1}(D)]_{a} \in T\text{.}\) The previous computation shows that \(([f]_{a})^{-1}(D) \in T\text{.}\) Hence \([f]_{a}\) is a \(T\) measurable function.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Monotone Class Theorem

    We have used the following strategy to prove that a \(\sigma\)-algebra has a certain property. First, find a collection of sets with that property which generates the \(\sigma\)-algebra, and second show that this collection of sets is itself a \(\sigma\)-algebra.
    🔗
    We used this strategy in showing that the cross sections of measurable sets are measurable. First we found a collection of subsets of \(X \times Y\) where the result held, and then we showed that the collection was a \(\sigma\)-algebra.
    🔗
    When we prove that the measure of a cross section is a measurable function, we will be unable to utilize this strategy. Instead we will need another technique that involves what are called monotone classes.
    🔗

    Definition 119. Set Algebra.

    Suppose \(W\) is a set and \(A\) is a set of subsets of \(W\text{.}\) Then \(A\) is called a set algebra, or simply, an algebra, on \(W\) if the following three conditions are satisfied.
    1. \(\displaystyle \emptyset \in A\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(E \in A\text{,}\) then \(W \setminus E \in A\) (closed under compliments)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. If \(E\) and \(F\) are elements of \(A\) then \(E \cup F \in A\text{.}\) (closed under finite unions)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The difference between algebras and \(\sigma\)-algebras is that \(\sigma\)-algebras are closed under countable unions. Every \(\sigma\)-algebra is an algebra, but not every algebra is a \(\sigma\)-algebra.
    🔗
    Example: Let \(W = \mathbf{R}\) and let \(A\) be the collection of sets with a finite number of elements or whose compliment has a finite number of elements. Then \(A\) is an algebra on \(W\text{,}\) but not a \(\sigma\)-algebra.
    🔗

    Theorem 120. Set of Finite Unions of Measurable Rectangles is an Algebra.

    Suppose \((X,S)\) and \((Y,T)\) are measurable spaces. Then
    1. The set of finite unions of measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\) is an algebra on \(X \times Y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Every finite union of measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\) can be written as a finite union of disjoint measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We will first prove (1) Let \(A\) denote the finite unions of measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\text{.}\) Since \(A\) is closed under finite unions and contains \(\emptyset\text{,}\) we just need to show that it is closed under compliments.
    🔗
    If \(A \in S\) and \(B \in T\) then
    \begin{equation*} (X \times Y) \setminus (A \times B) = \left( (X \setminus A) \times Y\right) \cup \left(X \times (Y \setminus B) \right) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The right hand side is a finite union of measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\) and thus belongs to \(A\text{.}\) Thus the compliment of a measurable rectangle in \(S \otimes T\) belongs to \(A\)
    🔗
    It follows from De Morgan’s laws that to show that \(A\) is closed under compliments, we need to show that \(A\) is closed under finite intersections.
    🔗
    Suppose \(A_{1}, \ldots A_{n},C_{1} \ldots C_{m} \in S\) and \(B_{1}, \ldots B_{n},D_{1} \ldots D_{m} \in T\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} \left( (A_1 \times B_1) \cup \ldots \cup (A_n \times B_n) \right) \cap \left((C_1 \times D_1) \cup \ldots \cup (C_m \times D_m)\right) \amp = \bigcup_{j=1}^n \bigcup_{k=1}^m \left((A_j \times B_j) \cap (C_k \times D_k) \right)\\ \amp = \bigcup_{j=1}^n \bigcup_{k=1}^m \left((A_j \cap C_k) \times (B_j \cap D_k) \right) \end{align*}
    🔗
    Since this is a finite union of measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\text{,}\) A is closed under finite intersections. Thus (1) is proved.
    🔗
    To prove (2) suppose that \(A \times B\) and \(C \times D\) are measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} (A \times B) \cup (C \times D) = (A \times B) \cup \left(C \times (D \setminus B)\right) \cup \left((C\setminus A) \cup (B \cap D) \right) \end{equation*}
    Thus a union of 2 measurable rectangles can be written as a disjoint union of three measurable rectangles.
    🔗
    If we have some arbitrary finite union of rectangles, and they are not disjoint, we can take a pair of non-disjoint rectangles and replace them with three disjoint rectangles. Repeating this process eventually results in a finite disjoint union of measurable rectangles.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 121. Monotone Class.

    Suppose \(W\) is a set and \(M\) is a set of subsets of \(W\text{.}\) Then \(M\) is called a monotone class on \(W\) if the following conditions are satisfied.
    1. If \(E_{1} \subseteq E_{2} \subseteq \ldots\) is an increasing sequence of sets in \(M\) then \(\displaystyle \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}E_{n} \in M\) (closed under countable increasing unions)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(E_{1} \supseteq E_{2} \supseteq \ldots\) is an decreasing sequence of sets in \(M\) then \(\displaystyle \bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}E_{n} \in M\) (closed under countable decreasing intersections)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Every \(\sigma\)-algebra is a monotone class, but not every monotone class is a \(\sigma\)-algebra, or even an algebra.
    🔗
    Example: Let \(A\) be the set of all intervals of \(\mathbf{R}\text{.}\) Then \(A\) is closed under countable increasing unions and countable decreasing intersections so \(A\) is a monotone class. However, \(A\) is not closed under compliments so \(A\) is not an algebra.
    🔗
    Example: Suppose \(A\) is a collection of some subsets of \(W\text{.}\) Then the intersection of all monotone classes of \(W\) containing \(A\) is a monotone class containing \(A\text{.}\) This is the smallest monotone class that contains \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 122. Monotone Class Theorem.

    Suppose \(\mathcal{A}\) is an algebra on a set \(W\text{.}\) Then the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{A}\) is the smallest monotone class containing \(\mathcal{A}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Intuitively this makes sense because a \(\sigma\)-algebra is an algebra that is closed under countable unions rather than finite unions, much like a monotone class.
    🔗

    Proof.

    Let \(\mathcal{M}\) denote the smallest monotone class containing \(\mathcal{A}\text{.}\) Since every \(\sigma\)-algebra is a monotone class, it follows that \(\mathcal{M}\) is contained in the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{A}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    It remains to prove that the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{A}\) is contained in \(\mathcal{M}\text{.}\) First suppose \(A \in \mathcal{A}\) and let \(\mathcal{E}= \{E \in \mathcal{M}: A \cup E \in M\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then \(\mathcal{A}\subseteq \mathcal{E}\) because the union of two sets in \(\mathcal{A}\) is still in \(\mathcal{A}\text{.}\) In fact you can verify that \(E\) is a monotone class that contains \(\mathcal{A}\text{.}\) Thus the smallest monotone class that contains \(\mathcal{A}\) is contained in \(\mathcal{E}\text{.}\) Thus \(M \subseteq \mathcal{E}\text{.}\) This proves that \(A \cup E \in M\) for every \(E \in \mathcal{M}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now let \(\mathcal{D}= \{D \in \mathcal{M}: D \cup E \in \mathcal{M}\text{ for all }E \in \mathcal{M}\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We just showed that \(A \cup E \in \mathcal{M}\) for all \(E \in M\text{.}\) By repeating the previous argument we may conclude that this holds for all \(A \in \mathcal{A}\) This shows that \(\mathcal{A}\subseteq \mathcal{D}\)
    🔗
    You can also verify that \(\mathcal{D}\) is a monotone class. Thus \(\mathcal{M}\subseteq \mathcal{D}\) and we have proved that \(D \cup E \in \mathcal{M}\) for all \(D,E \in \mathcal{M}\text{.}\) This can be generalized to show that \(\mathcal{M}\) is closed under finite unions.
    🔗
    In addition if \(E_{1}, E_{2}, \ldots \in \mathcal{M}\) then
    \begin{equation*} E_1 \cup E_2 \cup E_3 \cup \ldots = E_1 \cup (E_1 \cup E_2) \cup (E_1 \cup E_2 \cup E_3) \end{equation*}
    which is an increasing union of sets in \(\mathcal{M}\) and thus belongs to \(\mathcal{M}\text{.}\) Thus \(\mathcal{M}\) is closed under countable unions.
    🔗
    Finally let \(\mathcal{M}' = \{E \in \mathcal{M}: W \setminus E \in \mathcal{M}\}\text{.}\) Since \(\mathcal{A}\subseteq \mathcal{M}\) and \(A\) is closed under complements, \(\mathcal{A}\subseteq \mathcal{M}'\text{.}\) In addition one can verify that \(\mathcal{M}'\) is a monotone class. Thus \(\mathcal{M}\subseteq \mathcal{M}'\text{.}\) This means that \(M\) is closed under compliments.
    🔗
    We have shown that \(\mathcal{M}\) is closed under countable unions and complements. It also trivially contains \(\emptyset\text{.}\) Thus \(\mathcal{M}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra that contains \(\mathcal{A}\text{.}\) Thus \(\mathcal{M}\) is the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra that contains \(\mathcal{A}\text{,}\) which completes the proof.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Products of Measures

    Definition 123. Finite Measure.

    A measure \(\mu\) on a measurable space \((X,S)\) is said to be finite if \(\mu(X) < \infty\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 124.
    \(\sigma\)-Finite Measures
    🔗
    .

    A measure is called \(\sigma\)-finite if the whole space can be written as the countable union of sets with finite measures. In other words, a measure \(\mu\) on a measurable space \((X,S)\) is called \(\sigma\)-finite if there exists a sequence \(X_{1}\text{,}\) \(X_{2}, \ldots\) of sets in \(S\) such that
    \begin{equation*} X = \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} X_k \text{ and } \mu(X_k) < \infty \text{ for every } k \in \mathbf{N} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Example: Every probability measure on a probability space is a finite measure.
    🔗
    Example: Lebesgue measure on \([0,1]\) is a finite measure.
    🔗
    Example: Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbf{R}\) is not a finite measure, but it is a \(\sigma\)-finite measure.
    🔗
    Example: Counting measure on \(\mathbf{R}\) is not a \(\sigma\)-finite measure, since the countable union of finite sets is countable.
    🔗
    The next result will allow us to define product measures for finite measures.
    🔗

    Theorem 125. Measure of a Cross-Section is a Measurable Function.

    Suppose \((X,S,\mu)\) and \((Y,T, \nu)\) are measure spaces. If \(E \in S \otimes T\text{,}\) then
    1. \(x \to \nu([E]_{X})\) is an \(S\)-measurable function on \(X\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(y \to \mu([E]^{y})\) is a \(T\)-measurable function on \(Y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    It is this proof that requires the use of the Monotone Class Theorem.
    🔗

    Proof.

    We’ll prove (1). The proof of (2) is similar. If \(E \in S \otimes T\text{,}\) then \([E]_{x} \in T\) for every \(x \in X\) by the Theorem that cross sections of measurable sets are measurable. Thus the function \(x \to \nu([E]_{x})\) is an \(S\)-measurable function on \(X\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We first consider the case that \(\nu\) is a finite measure. Let
    \begin{equation*} M = \{E \in S \otimes T: x \to \nu([E]_x) \text{ is an -measurable function on }\} \end{equation*}
    We want to show that \(M = S \otimes T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(A \in S\) and \(B \in T\text{,}\) then one can show that \(\nu([A \times B]_{x}) = \nu(B) \chi_{A}(x)\) for every \(x \in X\text{.}\) Thus the function \(x \to \nu([A \times B]_{x})\) equals the function \(\nu(B) \chi_{A}(x)\) as a function of \(x\text{,}\) which is an \(S\)-measurable function on \(X\text{.}\) Thus \(M\) contains all measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(\mathcal{A}\) denote the set of finite unions of measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\) and suppose \(E \in \mathcal{A}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This means that \(E\) can be written as a finite union of disjoint measurable rectangles. That is,
    \begin{equation*} E = E_1 \cup E_2 \cup \ldots \cup E_n \end{equation*}
    where \(E_{1}, E_{2}, \ldots E_{n}\) are disjoint measurable rectangles.
    🔗
    Thus,
    \begin{align*} \nu([E]_x) \amp = \nu(E_1 \cup E_2 \cup \ldots \cup E_n)_x)\\ \amp = \nu([E_1]_x \cup [E_2]_x \cup \ldots \cup [E_n]_x) \\ \amp =\nu([E_1]_x) + \nu([E_2]_x) + \ldots + \nu([E_n]_x) \end{align*}
    where the last line follows because \(\nu\) is a measure and the sets are disjoint.
    🔗
    This shows that \(x \to \nu([E]_{x})\) is a finite sum of \(S\)-measurable functions and thus is an \(S\)-measurable function.
    🔗
    This proves that \(E \in M\) and hence \(\mathcal{A}\subseteq M\text{.}\) Now we will show that \(M\) is a monotone class so that we can apply the Monotone Class Theorem.
    🔗
    We will first show closure under increasing unions. Suppose \(E_{1} \subseteq E_{2} \subseteq \ldots\) is an increasing sequence of sets in \(M\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} \nu\left(\left[ \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} E_k \right]_x \right) \amp = \nu\left( \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} [E_k]_x \right) \\ \amp =\lim_{k\to \infty} \nu([E_k]_x) \end{align*}
    🔗
    Since the pointwise limit of \(S\)-measurable functions is \(S\)-measurable, this shows that \(x \to \nu\left(\left[ \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}E_{k} \right]_{x} \right)\) is an \(S\)-measurable function. Hence \(\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}E_{k} \in M\) and \(M\) is closed under increasing unions.
    🔗
    The proof that \(M\) is closed under decreasing intersections is similar. It is worth pointing out that in that proof we need to use the fact that \(\nu\) is a finite measure in order to show that the measure of a decreasing intersection is equal to taking the limit of the measures of each set. This shows that \(M\) is a monotone class that contains \(\mathcal{A}\text{,}\) the algebra of all finite unions of measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\text{.}\) Thus the monotone class theorem implies that \(M\) is the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{A}\text{.}\) Thus \(M\) contains \(S \otimes T\) which completes the proof in the case that \(\nu\) is a finite measure.
    🔗
    We now consider the case that \(\mu\) is a \(\sigma\)-finite measure. This means that there exists a sequence of sets \(Y_{1}, Y_{2}, \ldots \in T\) such that \(\displaystyle \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}Y_{k} = Y\) and \(\nu(Y_{k}) < \infty\) for each \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By replacing each \(Y_{k}\) with \(Y_{1} \cup \ldots \cup Y_{k}\text{,}\) we can create an increasing sequence \(Y_{1} \subseteq Y_{2} \subseteq \ldots\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(E \in S \otimes T\) then
    \begin{equation*} \nu([E]_x) = \lim_{k\to \infty} \nu([E\cap (X \times Y_k)]_x) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We can see that the function \(x \to \nu([E\cap (X \times Y_{k})]_{x})\) is an \(S\)-measurable function as follows by considering the finite measure obtained by restricting \(\nu\) to the \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(Y\) of sets that are contained in \(Y_{k}\text{.}\) The previous equation then implies that \(x \to \nu([E]_{x})\) is an \(S\)-measurable function on \(X\text{,}\) which completes the proof of \((1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 126. Integration Notation.

    Suppose that \((X,S,\mu)\) is a measure space and \(g:X \to [-\infty,\infty]\) is a function. The notation \(\displaystyle \int g(x) d\mu(x)\) means \(\displaystyle \int g d\mu\) where \(d\mu(x)\) indicates that all variables other than \(x\) should be treated as constant.
    🔗
    🔗
    Example: If \(\mu\) is Lebesgue measure on \([0,1]\) then
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 xy^2 -x d\mu(x) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 - \frac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 xy^2 -x d\mu(y) = \frac{1}{3}x-x = \frac{-2}{3}x \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Definition 127. Iterated Integrals.

    Suppose \((X,S,\mu)\) and \((T,Y,\nu)\) are measure spaces and \(f: X \times Y \to \mathbf{R}\) is a function. Then
    \begin{equation*} \int_X \int_Y f(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) \text{ means } \int_x \left( \int_Y f(x,y) d\nu(y) \right) d\mu(x) \end{equation*}
    In other words, first fix \(x\) and integrate \(f(x,y)\) as a function of \(y\text{,}\) then fix \(y\) and integrate the output as a function of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Example: If \(mu\) denotes Lebesgue measure on \([0,1]\) then
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 \int_0^1 xy^2 -x d\mu(y) d\mu(x) = \int_0^1 \frac{-2}{3}x d\mu(x) = \frac{-1}{3} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 \int_0^1 xy^2-x d\mu(x) d\mu(y) = \int_0^1 \frac{1}{2}y^2 - \frac{1}{2} d\mu(y) = \frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{2} = \frac{-1}{3} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Definition 128. Product of a Measure.

    Suppose \((X,S,\mu)\) and \((Y,T,\nu)\) are \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces. For \(E \in S\otimes T\) define \((\mu \times \nu)(E)\) by
    \begin{equation*} (\mu \times \nu)(E) = \int_X \int_Y \chi_E(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This integral is well defined because the inner integral is equal to \(\nu([E]_{x}) \in T\) and the function \(x \to \nu([E]_{x})\) is \(S\)-measurable by the previous theorem.
    🔗
    Example: Suppose \((X,S,\mu)\) and \((Y,T,\nu)\) are \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces. If \(A \in S\) and \(B \in T\) then
    \begin{align*} (\mu \times \nu)(A\times B) \amp = \int_X \int_Y \chi_{A \times B}(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x)\\ \amp =\int_X \chi_{A} \nu(B) d\mu(x) \\ \amp = \mu(A) \nu(B) \end{align*}
    🔗
    Thus the product measure of a measurable rectangle is the product of the measures of the corresponding sets.
    🔗

    Theorem 129. Product of Two Measures is a Measure.

    Suppose \((X,S,\mu)\) and \((Y,T,\nu)\) are \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces. Then \(\mu \times \nu\) is a measure on \((X \times Y, S\otimes T)\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Since \((\mu \times \nu)(\emptyset) = 0\) we just need to show countable additivity. Suppose \(E_{1}, E_{2}, \ldots\) is a disjoint sequence of sets in \(S \otimes T\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} (\mu \times \nu)\left( \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} E_k \right) \amp = \int_X \int_Y \chi_{(\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} E_k)} d\nu(y) d\mu(x) \\ \amp = \int_X \nu \left( \left[ \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} E_k \right]_x \right) d\mu(x) \\ \amp = \int_X \nu \left( \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} [E_k ]_x \right) d\mu(x)\\ \amp = \int_X \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \nu([E_k]_x) d\mu(x) \end{align*}
    🔗
    Since the partial sums of \(\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu([E_{k}]_{x})\) form an increasing sequence of non-negative measurable functions, the Monotone Convergence Theorem allows us to interchange the sum and the integral. Hence,
    \begin{align*} \int_X \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \nu([E_k]_x) d\mu(x)\amp = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \int_X \nu([E_k]_x) d\mu(x) \\ \amp = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (\mu \times \nu) (E_k). \end{align*}
    which shows that \((\mu\times \nu)\) is countably additive.
