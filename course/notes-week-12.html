    Section Week 12: Iterated Integrals (5B)

    Subsection Tonelli’s Theorem

    In the previous section we saw an example in which the order of integration of an iterated integral did not affect the final answer. The next theorem tells us that the order of integration can be switched if the function being integrated is non-negative and the measure is \(\sigma\)-finite.
    🔗

    Theorem 130. Tonelli’s Theorem.

    Suppose \((X,S,\mu)\) and \((Y,T,\nu)\) are \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces. Suppose \(f: X \times Y \to [0,\infty]\) is \(S\otimes T\) measurable. Then
    1. \(x \to \int_{Y} f(x,y) d\nu(y)\) is an \(S\)-measurable function on \(X\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(y \to \int_{X} f(x,y) d\mu(x)\) is a \(T\)-measurable function on \(Y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \begin{align*} \int_{X \times Y} f(x,y) d(\mu \times \nu) \amp = \int_X \int_Y f(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x)\\ \amp = \int_Y \int_X f(x,y) d\mu(x) d\nu(y) \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We start by proving the theorem for characteristic functions. Suppose that \(f = \chi_{E}\) where \(E \in S \otimes T\text{.}\) In this case
    \begin{equation*} \int_Y \chi_E(x,y) d\nu(y) = \nu([E]_x) \end{equation*}
    for every \(x \in X\) and
    \begin{equation*} \int_X \chi_E(x,y) d\mu(x) = \mu([E]^y) \end{equation*}
    for every \(y \in Y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thus (1) and (2) hold because the measure of a cross-section is a measurable function. To prove (3) we first suppose that \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are finite measures and define
    \begin{equation*} \left\{ E \in S \otimes T: \int_X \int_Y \chi_E(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) = \int_Y \int_X \chi_E(x,y) d\mu(x) d\nu(y) \right\} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If \(A \in S\) and \(B \in T\) then \(A \times B \in M\) because both sides of the equation defining \(M\) are equal to \(\mu(A) \mu(B)\text{.}\) Let \(\mathcal{A}\) denote the set of finite unions of measurable rectangles in \(S \otimes T\text{.}\) Because every finite union of measurable rectangles can be written as a disjoint finite union of measurable rectangles, every element of \(\mathcal{A}\) is a disjoint union of rectangles in \(S \otimes T\text{.}\) It thus follows that \(\mathcal{A}\subseteq M\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(E \subseteq F\) then \(\chi_{E} \leq \chi_{F}\text{.}\) Hence it follows from the Monotone Convergence Theorem that \(M\) is closed under increasing unions.
    🔗
    If \(E \supseteq F\) then \(\chi_{E}\) and \(\chi_{F}\) are both bounded. Hence it follows from the Bounded Convergence Theorem that \(M\) is closed under decreasing intersections. (This is where we use the assumption that \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are finite measures.)
    🔗
    We have shown that \(M\) is a monotone class that contains the algebra \(A\) of all finite unions of measurable rectangles in \(S\otimes T\text{.}\) Thus by the Monotone Class Theorem \(M\) contains the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This means that \(M\) contains \(S \otimes T\) and hence
    \begin{equation*} \int_X \int_Y \chi_E(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) = \int_Y \int_X \chi_E(x,y) d\mu(x) d\nu(y) \end{equation*}
    for all \(E \in S \otimes T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now suppose that \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are merely \(\sigma\)-finite measures. Write \(X\) as an increasing union of sets \(X_{1} \subseteq X_{2} \subseteq \ldots\) in \(S\) with finite measures and write \(Y\) as an increasing union of sets \(Y_{1} \subseteq Y_{2} \subseteq \ldots\) in \(T\) with finite measures.
    🔗
    Suppose \(E \in S \otimes T\text{.}\) Then it follows from the finite measure case that
    \begin{equation*} \int_X \int_Y \chi_{E \cap (X_j \times Y_k)}(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) = \int_Y \int_X \chi_{E \cap (X_j \times Y_k)}(x,y) d\mu(x) d\nu(y) \end{equation*}
    for all \(j,k \in \mathbf{N}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We can hold \(k\) fixed and apply the Monotone Convergence Theorem to \(j\) to conclude that
    \begin{equation*} \int_X \int_Y \chi_{E \cap (X \times Y_k)}(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) = \int_Y \int_X \chi_{E \cap (X \times Y_k)}(x,y) d\mu(x) d\nu(y) \end{equation*}
    for all \(k \in \mathbf{N}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then we can apply the Monotone Convergence Theorem to \(k\) to show that
    \begin{equation*} \int_X \int_Y \chi_{E \cap (X \times Y)}(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) = \int_Y \int_X \chi_{E \cap (X \times Y)}(x,y) d\mu(x) d\nu(y) \end{equation*}
    for all \(E \in S\otimes T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now suppose that \(f: X \times Y \to [0,\infty]\) is an arbitrary \(S \otimes T\)-measurable function. Define a sequence \(f_{1}, f_{2}, \ldots\) of simple \(S\)-measurable functions from \(X \times Y\) to \([0, \infty]\) by
    \begin{equation*} f_k(x,y) = \begin{cases} \hfill \frac{m}{2^k} \amp \text{ if and with } \\ \hfill k \hfill \amp \text{ if } \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We observe that \(f_{1}(x,y) \leq f_{2}(x,y) \leq \ldots\) and that \(\displaystyle \lim_{k \to \infty}f_{k}(x,y) = f(x,y)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For each \(k\text{,}\) \(f_{k}(x,y)\) is a simple function. Specifically it is a function of the form \(c_{k} \chi_{E_k}\) where \(E_{k} \in S \otimes T\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{equation*} \int_X \int_Y f_k(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) = \int_Y \int_X f_k(x,y) d\mu(x) d\nu(y) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(f_{k}(x,y)\) is an increasing sequence of functions, it follows from the Monotone Convergence Theorem that
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{k\to \infty} \int_Y f_k(x,y) d\nu(y) = \int_Y f(x,y) d\nu(y) \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{k\to \infty} \int_X f_k(x,y) d\mu(x) = \int_X f(x,y) d\mu(x) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus the function \(x \to \int_{Y} f(x,y) d\nu(y)\) is a pointwise limit of \(S\)-measurable functions and is thus an \(S\)-measurable function.
    🔗
    Likewise the function \(y \to \int_{X} f(x,y) d\mu(x)\) is a pointwise limit of \(T\)-measurable functions and is thus a \(T\)-measurable function. Thus applying the Monotone Convergence Theorem to both sides of the previous equation we obtain that
    \begin{equation*} \int_X \int_Y f(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) = \int_Y \int_X f(x,y) d\mu(x) d\nu(y) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    By applying Tonell’s Theorem to the counting measure we get the following corollary that allows us to switch the order of summation of a double sum.
    🔗

    Corollary 131. Double Sum of Non-negative numbers.

    If \(\{x_{jk}: j,k \in \mathbf{Z}\}\) is a doubly indexed collection of non-negative numbers, then
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} x_{jk} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} x_{jk} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Fubini’s Theorem

    Tonelli’s Theorem has the drawback that the function being integrated be non-negative. Fubini’s Theorem has the same conclusion as Tonelli’s Theorem but only requires that the integral of the absolute value be finite, which makes it more practical in many situations.
    🔗
    Historically, Fubini’s Theorem was proven before Tonell’s Theorem, but we present Tonelli’s Theorem first because we can make use of it to greatly simplify the proof of Fubini’s Theorem.
    🔗

    Theorem 132. Fubini’s Theorem.

    Suppose \((X,S,\mu)\) and \((Y,T, \nu)\) are measure spaces. Suppose \(f: X \times Y \to [-\infty, \infty]\) is an \(S\)-measurable function and \(\int_{X \times Y}|f| d(\mu \times \nu) < \infty\text{.}\) Then
    1. \(\int_{Y} |f(x,y)| d\nu(y) < \infty\) for almost every \(x \in X\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\int_{X} |f(x,y)| d\mu(x) < \infty\) for almost every \(y \in Y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(x \to \int_{Y} f(x,y) d\nu(y)\) is an \(S\)-measurable function on \(X\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(y \to \int_{X} f(x,y) d\mu(x)\) is a \(T\)-measurable function on \(Y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \begin{align*} \int_{X \times Y} f(x,y) d(\mu \times \nu) \amp = \int_X \int_Y f(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) \\ \amp = \int_Y \int_X f(x,y) d\mu(x) d\nu(y) \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    By applying Tonelli’s Theorem to the non-negative function \(|f|\) we conclude that \(x \to \int_Y |f(x,y)| d\nu(y)\) is an \(S\)-measurable function on \(X\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} \left\{ x \in X: \int_Y |f(x,y)| d\nu(y) = \infty \right\} \in S \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Tonelli’s Theorem applied to \(|f|\) also implies that
    \begin{equation*} \int_X \int_Y |f(x,y)| d\nu(y) d\mu(x) < \infty \end{equation*}
    since the above integrated integral is equal to \(\int_{X\times Y}|f| d (\mu \times \nu)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} \mu \left(\left\{ x \in X: \int_Y |f(x,y)| d\nu(y) = \infty \right\} \right) = 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence by Tonelli’s Theorem
    \begin{equation*} x \to \int_{Y} f^+(x,y) d\nu(y) \text{ and } x \to \int_Y f^-(x,y) d\nu(y) \end{equation*}
    are \(S\)-measurable functions from \(X \to [0,\infty]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since \(f^{+} \leq |f|\) and \(f^{-} \leq |f|\) it follows that the sets \(\{x \in X: \int_{Y} f^{+}(x,y) d\nu(y) = \infty\}\) and \(\{x \in X: \int_{Y} f^{-}(x,y) d\nu(y) = \infty\}\) both have \(\mu\)-measure \(0\text{.}\) The intersection of these sets, which is the set of \(x \in X\) so that \(\int_{Y} f(x,y) d\nu\) is undefined also has \(\mu\)-measure \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This means that by redefining \(f\) to be \(0\) when both \(\int_{Y} f^{+}(x,y) d\nu(y)\) and \(\int_{Y} f^{+}(x,y) d\nu(y) = \infty\) (but still calling it \(f\) ) we obtain that \(\int_{Y} f(x,y) d\nu(y)\) is an \(S\)-measurable function.
    🔗
    Now
    \begin{align*} \amp \int_{X \times Y} f(x,y) d( \mu \times \nu) = \int_{X \times Y} f^+(x,y) d( \mu \times \nu)- \int_{X \times Y} f^-(x,y) d( \mu \times \nu) \\ \amp =\int_X \int_Y f^+(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) - \int_X \int_Y f^-(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) \\ \amp = \int_X \int_Y f^+(x,y) - f^-(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x)\\ \amp = \int_X \int_Y f(x,y) d\nu(y) d\mu(x) \end{align*}
    where we have used Tonelli’s theorem in the second line.
    🔗
    The same argument with \(x\) and \(y\) switched proves the other equality in Fubini’s Theorem.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Area Under the Graph

    In Calculus you learned that the area under the graph of a continuous function is equal to the (Riemann) integral of the function. We now show that the same result is true for the Lebesgue integral of measurable functions.
    🔗

    Definition 133. Region under the Graph.

    Suppose \(X\) is a set and \(f:X \to [0,\infty]\) is a function. Then the region under the graph of \(f\text{,}\) denoted \(U_{f}\text{,}\) is defined by
    \begin{equation*} U_f = \{(x,t) \in X \times (0,\infty): 0 < t < f(x)\} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Note that \(f\) need not be continuous in the previous definition. Thus we can define the region under a graph even for nowhere continuous functions such as Dirichlet’s function.
    🔗

    Theorem 134. Area under the Graph of a Function Equals the Integral.

    Suppose \((X,S,\mu)\) is a \(\sigma\)-finite measure space and \(f:X \to [0,\infty]\) is an \(S\)-measurable function. Let \(B\) denote the \(\sigma\)-algebra of Borel subsets of \((0,\infty)\) and let \(\lambda\) denote Lebesgue measure on \(((0,\infty),B)\text{.}\) Then \(U_{f} \in S \otimes B\) and
    \begin{equation*} (\mu \times \lambda)(U_f) = \int_X f d\mu = \int_{(0,\infty)} \mu(\{x\in X: t < f(x)\}) d\lambda(t) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    For \(k \in \mathbf{N}\text{,}\) define
    \begin{equation*} E_k = \bigcup_{m=0}^{k^2-1} \left( f^{-1}\left(\left[\frac{m}{k}, \frac{m+1}{k}\right)\right) \times \left(0, \frac{m}{k}\right)\right) \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} F_k = f^{-1}([k,\infty]) \times (0,k) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The sets inside the union in \(E_{k}\) are products consisting of an inverse image of a set that makes up a partition of \([0,k)\) multiplied by an interval (in \(Y\) ) with height equal to the infimum of the set. \(F_{k}\) is the inverse image of the interval \([k,\infty]\) multiplied by an interval (in \(Y\) ) with height equal to \(k\text{.}\) Note that \(E_{k}\) is a finite union of \(S \otimes B\) measurable rectangles and \(F_{k}\) is an \(S \otimes B\) measurable rectangle.
    🔗
    Since
    \begin{equation*} U_f = \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} (E_k \cup F_k) \end{equation*}
    \(U_{f}\) is in \(S \otimes B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By the definition of product measure
    \begin{align*} (\mu \times \lambda) (U_f) \amp = \int_X \int_{(0,\infty)} \chi_{U_f}(x,t) d\lambda(t) d\mu(x) \\ \amp = \int f(x) d\mu(x). \end{align*}
    This is because we are integrating the characteristic function of the region under the graph in \(y\text{,}\) which gives us the height of the graph at \(x\text{,}\) which is precisely \(f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By Tonelli’s theorem we can interchange the order of integration in the above double integral. Hence
    \begin{align*} (\mu \times \lambda) (U_t) \amp = \int_{(0,\infty)} \int_X \chi_{U_f}(x,t) d\mu(x) d\lambda(t) \\ \amp = \int_{(0,\infty)} \mu(\{x \in X: t < f(x)\}) d\lambda(t). \end{align*}
    This is because we are integrating the characteristic function of the region under the graph in \(x\) which gives us the measure of the set of \(x\) where \(t< f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hence we have verified both equalities in the statement of the theorem.
    🔗
    🔗
    Recall that Markov’s inequality states that
    \begin{equation*} \mu( \{x \in X: f(x) > t\}) \leq \frac{1}{t} \int |f| d\mu \end{equation*}
    for all \(t > 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    When \(\int f d\mu < \infty\) the second equality is a stronger version of Markov’s inequality since \(\int_{(0,\infty)}\frac{1}{t}dt = \infty\text{.}\)
